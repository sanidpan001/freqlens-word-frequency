Keyword Density & Word Frequency Analysis: The Complete Guide

Every piece of writing has a fingerprint: the pattern of words it repeats. A word frequency analyzer reads that fingerprint for you. Paste an article into the tool above and, in a fraction of a second, you see exactly which words and phrases dominate your text, how often each one appears, and what percentage of the whole each term represents. For bloggers, SEO writers, students, editors, and content marketers, that snapshot answers the two questions that matter most: am I saying what I think I am saying? and am I repeating my target keyword the right number of times?

This guide explains how keyword density works, what the safe-density bands mean, why bigrams and trigrams matter, and how to use frequency data to write content that reads naturally and ranks well.

What is keyword density?

Keyword density is the percentage of times a word or phrase appears in a text relative to the total number of words. The formula is simple:

density % = (occurrences of the term ÷ total words) × 100

If your 1,000-word article uses the phrase "email marketing" 12 times, its density is 1.2%. A keyword density checker automates this arithmetic for every term in the document at once, so instead of counting by hand you get a ranked table: term, count, and density percentage, side by side.

Density is computed on the full raw word count — including the small filler words — because that is the convention search engines and classic SEO tools have used for years. It makes densities comparable across documents of different lengths.

The safe-density bands: under, optimal, stuffed

Raw percentages are only useful with guidance, so FreqLens classifies every term into one of three bands:

  • Low (under 1%). The term is a minor presence in the text. That is perfectly fine for secondary topics — not every idea needs to dominate. But if your primary keyword sits here, search engines may not clearly understand what the page is about, and readers may not either.
  • Optimal (1–3%). The widely cited comfort zone for a focus keyword. The term appears often enough to establish the topic clearly, but not so often that the prose feels forced. Most well-edited articles land their main keyword in this band naturally.
  • Stuffed (over 3%). The term is repeated excessively. This is the classic signature of keyword stuffing — cramming a keyword into every other sentence in hopes of ranking higher. Modern search engines penalize this behavior, and human readers find it tedious. If your head term is stuffed, rewrite with synonyms, pronouns, and restructured sentences.

These bands are guidance, not law. A short 150-word product blurb will show spikier densities than a 3,000-word guide, and brand names or technical terms legitimately repeat more. Use the bands as a starting point, then apply editorial judgment.

Why a frequency analyzer beats a plain word counter

A word counter tells you how much you wrote: total words, characters, sentences. A word frequency counter tells you what you wrote. That distinction matters in at least four workflows:

  • SEO editing. Confirm your target keyword — and its close variants — appear in the optimal band before publishing, and catch accidental stuffing early.
  • Competitor analysis. Paste a rival's top-ranking article and see which terms and phrases it leans on. You are not copying; you are mapping the vocabulary the topic demands.
  • Academic writing. Spot overused crutch words ("however," "moreover," "very") and check that key terminology is used consistently rather than drifting between synonyms.
  • Editing and proofreading. Frequency tables expose repetition your eyes glide over: the same adjective three times in one paragraph, or a phrase you lean on unconsciously.

Bigrams and trigrams: phrases carry the meaning

Single words only tell half the story. The phrases "keyword density" and "density keyword" contain the same words but mean different things — order and pairing matter. That is why FreqLens also reports bigrams (two-word phrases) and trigrams (three-word phrases) with their own counts and density percentages.

Phrase frequency is where SEO writers find the real insights. Your article might use the word "coffee" at a healthy 1.5%, while the bigram "cold brew coffee" sits at 2.8% — revealing that one specific subtopic is doing the heavy lifting. Trigrams like "best budget espresso machine" expose the long-tail phrases your draft naturally targets, which is gold for headline and subheading choices.

Note that the tool builds phrases from the text after stop-words are removed, so you see meaningful content phrases ("email marketing software") rather than noise ("of the and").

Stop words: filtering the noise

Stop words are the grammatical glue of a language — the, and, of, to, in, is — words so common they carry almost no topical meaning. Any honest frequency table must filter them, or the top ten results of every analysis would be identical and useless.

FreqLens ships with a curated default stop-word list, but language is personal: a legal writer may want to filter "hereinafter," a medical writer "patient." The built-in stop-word editor lets you add or remove words with one click and reset to the default at any time. Your custom list is saved in your browser, so your next analysis uses your rules automatically.

Reading-level stats: ease, grade, and time

Frequency is about what you say; readability is about who can follow it. Alongside the tables, FreqLens reports three classic reading metrics:

  • Flesch Reading Ease (0–100). Higher is easier. Scores of 60–70 suit a general audience; 30–50 reads as academic or technical.
  • Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level. The US school grade needed to understand the text. Most web content aims for grade 8–10.
  • Estimated reading time, based on an average adult pace of 200 words per minute — handy for labeling articles ("6 min read").

Pair these with the frequency report for a full editorial check: if reading ease is low and a jargon term is stuffed, you have both a clarity and a repetition problem to fix.

Common keyword density mistakes

  • Chasing an exact percentage. There is no magic number that guarantees rankings. The 1–3% band is a sanity check, not a target to hit precisely.
  • Ignoring variants. Search engines understand synonyms and plurals. If "running shoes," "runners," and "sneakers" each sit at 1%, your topic density is far higher than any single row suggests.
  • Forgetting short texts. In a 100-word paragraph, one repeated word hits 3% fast. Bands are most meaningful on texts of a few hundred words or more.
  • Optimizing only for robots. A paragraph that reads awkwardly to a human will not convert, no matter how perfect its density table looks.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good keyword density for SEO?

There is no single magic number, but the widely used guideline is 1–3% for your primary keyword: often enough to establish the topic clearly, but not so often that the text feels forced. FreqLens marks this range as the "Optimal" band. Anything consistently above 3% risks looking like keyword stuffing to both readers and search engines.

How is keyword density calculated?

Keyword density = (number of times the term appears ÷ total words in the text) × 100. For example, a keyword used 15 times in a 1,000-word article has a density of 1.5%. FreqLens computes this automatically for every word, bigram, and trigram in your text, using the full raw word count as the denominator — the standard SEO convention.

What is the difference between a word counter and a word frequency analyzer?

A word counter tells you how much you wrote — totals like words, characters, and sentences. A word frequency analyzer tells you what you wrote: a ranked breakdown of each word and phrase, how often it appears, and its density percentage. Frequency analysis is what SEO writers and editors use to check keyword usage and spot unconscious repetition.

Why do bigrams and trigrams matter for content analysis?

Single words lack context — "content" could mean anything, but the bigram "content marketing" and the trigram "content marketing strategy" reveal real topics. Phrase frequency shows which multi-word ideas dominate your draft, helps you discover natural long-tail phrases for subheadings, and catches phrase-level stuffing that a single-word table would miss.

Can keyword stuffing hurt my rankings?

Yes. Repeating a keyword excessively — typically above 3% density, or crammed unnaturally into sentences — is a known spam signal. Search engines may demote pages that do it, and human readers bounce from text that reads robotically. If FreqLens flags your main term as "Stuffed," rewrite with synonyms, pronouns, and varied sentence structure.
